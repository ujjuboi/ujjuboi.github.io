## Title: Teaching Machines to Read Legal Documents

## Date: August 10, 2026

## Excerpt: Building an NLP pipeline that turns PDFs, DOCs, and DOCX legal files into structured JSON — section extraction with SpaCy, multi-format parsing, and the dataset problem that stalled our custom NER model.

## Paragraphs

- <p>Legal documents have a way of actively resisting structure. Court judgments, appeals, petitions — each one arrives in a different template, a different format, with its own idea of where the parties end and the submissions begin. Anyone who has had to manually inspect a stack of these documents knows how slow and error-prone the process is. So I built a tool with one goal: take any legal document and emit structured JSON capturing its key features and entities, so the information inside can be consumed programmatically instead of skimmed by eye.</p>
- <p>The architecture stayed deliberately simple: three small modules, each with a single responsibility. <code>parseDocument.py</code> is the orchestrator — it scans a folder of incoming documents, dispatches each to the right converter based on its extension, feeds the extracted text through the section detector, and writes one <code>.json</code> file per input document. <code>fileConverter.py</code> knows nothing about law; it only knows how to get text out of files. <code>sections.py</code> knows nothing about file formats; it only knows how to find structure in lines of text.</p>
- <p>The converter layer turned out to be its own little museum of format quirks. PDFs go through pdfminer, walking page by page with a <code>PDFPageInterpreter</code> and a <code>TextConverter</code>, accumulating text across the whole document. Modern Word files (<code>.docx</code>) are handled by docx2txt, legacy <code>.doc</code> files fall back to the textract library, and plain text files are read directly. The critical design decision was normalizing everything into a common shape — a full text string plus a list of lines — so the downstream NLP layer never needed to care where the document came from. Working with real-world files taught me quickly how inconsistent exports are: stray tabs from Word, page-break artifacts in PDFs, encoding oddities in old formats.</p>
- <p>The heart of the system is section detection. I curated a vocabulary of headings that appear in legal paperwork — "date of hearing," "date of judgment," "solicitor," "counsel," "parties," "submission," statute references like IPC and FCA, and court names — alongside general structural headings like "background" and "conclusion." Every line in the document goes through the same pipeline: lowercase it, run it through SpaCy for lemmatization, tokenize it with NLTK, and strip stopwords like "of," "and," and "&." If the surviving tokens are three or fewer and the result matches a known heading, that line marks a section boundary.</p>
- <p>The normalization step matters more than it looks. "Awards & Achievements" and "awards achievements" collapse to the same form; singular and plural headings unify under lemmatization. The three-token length guard is equally important — it stops sentences that merely contain words like "court" or "judge" from falsely splitting the document mid-narrative. Once the boundaries are marked, all the lines between two boundaries are joined into a single clean paragraph (empty lines and dash separators stripped) and stored under their heading's key.</p>
- <p>The output is one JSON file per document: keys are section names, values are the corresponding paragraphs. A consumer asking for the "reason" section of a judgment gets it directly, without ever touching raw text parsing again. It's a small capability, but it completely changes what downstream automation can build on top of the corpus.</p>
- <p>I'll be honest about what we didn't achieve, because it shaped the whole project. The original plan was to train our own named entity recognition model in SpaCy 3.0 — tagging judges, counsel, statutes, and cited cases automatically. That requires an annotated corpus: text plus character-offset spans marking each entity. We found the UCI Legal Case Reports dataset, and while converting it into SpaCy's training format (a tuple of text and entity spans, serialized to pickle) was mechanically straightforward, the dataset simply wasn't elaborate enough for a robust model. Rather than ship a weak NER component, we shipped the deterministic pipeline and documented the exact training format for whoever picks the model work up later.</p>
- <p>The biggest lessons from this project: reach for rules and classical NLP before deep learning, especially when your labeled data is thin; data availability is the real constraint on ML ambitions, not model architecture; and nothing teaches you about messy real-world data faster than parsing hundreds of documents nobody standardized. The whole pipeline is open-sourced on my GitHub — sometimes the most valuable tool isn't the cleverest model, it's the boring reliable one that turns chaos into JSON.</p>
